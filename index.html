A note on data reliability

Two things worth knowing before trusting any single month's number too much. First, the 2025 government shutdown caused BLS to skip publishing October and November 2025 CPI data entirely — those gaps distort some of the year-over-year comparisons still working through the system in 2026. Second, a federal watchdog report found that while BLS "took steps to fix data release failures," it concluded more safeguards are still needed — a reminder that even official releases carry some operational risk right now. None of this means the numbers below are wrong, just that they deserve the usual caution around revisions (CPI data can be revised for up to 5 years; jobs data gets revised monthly, sometimes by over 100,000 as happened with May/June 2026).

Update, October 22: the 2027 Social Security COLA is 3.6%. The Social Security Administration announced on October 14 that benefits will rise 3.6% in 2027 (SSA COLA summary); CNBC reported it as the largest increase in four years. A correction to earlier text on this page: the COLA is based on the CPI-W (the index for urban wage earners and clerical workers), not the Chained CPI. The Chained CPI (C-CPI-U) is the index used to index federal income-tax brackets and the standard deduction. It is designed to run slightly below headline CPI because it accounts for consumers substituting cheaper goods when prices rise. The September C-CPI-U is now verified: BLS reported on October 14 that it rose 3.5% over 12 months (and 0.5% for the month, not seasonally adjusted), against 3.7% for the headline CPI-U and 3.9% for the CPI-W (BLS CPI release, September 2026). C-CPI-U figures are preliminary and BLS revises them, so the 3.5% may change.
Did the earlier version of this dashboard call the COLA correctly? Partly. Earlier versions said the chained CPI (then 3.3% for July) "points toward a larger-than-average COLA bump for 2027." They gave no number, so there is no numeric forecast to score. The qualitative call held up against most benchmarks: the 3.6% COLA is 0.8 points above 2026's 2.8% (American Action Forum), and Yahoo Finance reports it as the highest in four years and well above the 2.6% average of the past two decades (Yahoo Finance). It was not above every benchmark: AAF notes it is slightly below the 3.7% average since 1975, and the two sources give different long-run averages, so "larger than average" depends on the period chosen. Two further problems with the earlier text: it used the wrong index (the COLA follows the CPI-W, not the chained CPI, corrected above), and the chained CPI understated the move. By September the C-CPI-U was 3.5% and the CPI-W 3.9%. The exact COLA is the percentage rise in the CPI-W's July-to-September average over the prior year's; I did not recompute it from index values.
What this means for youIf you or someone you support relies on Social Security, the 3.6% COLA is now set for 2027. Compare it with your own expenses: it is meant to match price increases for a typical household, and gas, medical and housing costs may weigh differently for you. See the Medicare section for the Part B premium that will be deducted from many checks.

The uninsured rate was essentially flat in 2025, but CDC and CBO both expect it to rise going forward. Enhanced ACA marketplace subsidies expired at the end of 2025, and Medicaid eligibility changes from last year's federal tax and spending law are set to phase in — the Congressional Budget Office projects roughly 10 million more uninsured Americans over the next decade as a result.
CDC detail (May 28 release): in the National Health Interview Survey, 8.3% of people of all ages (28.0 million) were uninsured in 2025, down from 9.2% in 2021. Among adults 18-64 it was 11.6%, unchanged from 2024. For children it was 5.6% (4.0 million), a small rise from 5.1% that CDC says is not statistically significant.
