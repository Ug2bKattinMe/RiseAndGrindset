A note on data reliability

Two things worth knowing before trusting any single month's number too much. First, the 2025 government shutdown caused BLS to skip publishing October and November 2025 CPI data entirely — those gaps distort some of the year-over-year comparisons still working through the system in 2026. Second, a federal watchdog report found that while BLS "took steps to fix data release failures," it concluded more safeguards are still needed — a reminder that even official releases carry some operational risk right now. None of this means the numbers below are wrong, just that they deserve the usual caution around revisions (CPI data can be revised for up to 5 years; jobs data gets revised monthly, sometimes by over 100,000 as happened with May/June 2026).

This is the measure that's easy to miss but directly affects money in people's pockets. The Chained CPI (C-CPI-U) — not the headline CPI everyone talks about — is what the IRS uses to set tax bracket thresholds and standard deductions each year, and a closely related index sets the annual Social Security Cost-of-Living Adjustment (COLA). It's designed to run slightly below headline CPI because it accounts for consumers substituting cheaper goods when prices rise (e.g., chicken instead of beef). At 3.3%, it's tracking close to headline CPI right now, which points toward a larger-than-average COLA bump for 2027 if the pattern holds through the fall reference period the Social Security Administration uses.
What this means for youIf you or someone you support relies on Social Security, this number (not headline CPI) is what determines next year's benefit increase. At 3.3%, expect a larger-than-typical COLA bump announced this October.

Wildfires don't move the national CPI the way oil shocks do, but they're a real, growing, and localized cost pressure — economists are calling the phenomenon "climateflation." The January 2025 Palisades and Eaton fires destroyed over 16,000 structures and produced roughly $40 billion in insured losses; nearly 400,000 California home insurance policies have been canceled since 2021, and FAIR Plan (the state's insurer of last resort) enrollment is up 43% in 15 months. Rebuilding costs are compounding the problem: construction material and skilled-labor shortages pushed residential construction wages up more than 9% in 2025, far outpacing overall inflation. The 2026 fire season is already tracking worse than most recent years, with Florida and Georgia in extreme drought and Colorado closing its worst snowpack year on record — meaning this cost pressure is not behind us. It shows up in this dashboard mainly through shelter and insurance costs in fire-prone regions, not the national CPI headline.

Important methodology note: BLS does not price health insurance policies directly. It uses an "indirect method" — measuring insurers' retained earnings (premiums minus benefits paid) rather than premium prices themselves — because it can't reliably control for changes in plan quality across insurers. There is no standalone monthly "health insurance inflation rate" published the way there is for, say, groceries or gasoline. This is one of the steepest SNAP enrollment drops in about three decades — comparable to the 1996 welfare reform cuts. The cause is the "One Big Beautiful Bill" (H.R. 1), signed July 2025, which expanded work requirements and tightened eligibility, including for some immigrants with lawful status. The decline accelerated sharply after the law took effect: only about 744,000 people lost benefits in the first six months, versus millions in the months since. It's landed in every state except Alaska, with drops of 10%+ in 23 states.
The cause is genuinely disputed. USDA Secretary Brooke Rollins has attributed much of the decline to a strong economy and reduced fraud. Food-security researchers, and the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities (a left-leaning think tank that produces the most detailed state-level tracking), attribute it primarily to the program becoming harder to access — more paperwork, stricter work-requirement verification. This dashboard can't adjudicate that dispute, but the enrollment numbers themselves are drawn from USDA administrative data and aren't contested.
Why it belongs here: SNAP covers roughly 1 in 8 Americans, and its recipients are disproportionately exposed to the food-at-home inflation tracked in the Groceries section above. Losing benefits while grocery CPI is still running at 3.0% year-over-year compounds financial pressure for millions of households in a way the topline inflation numbers don't capture.

People feel worse about the economy than most of the hard data alone would suggest, and it's getting worse, not better. This index has now fallen for three straight months (51.7 in August, 48.1 in September, 46.3 in the October preliminary) to a five-month low, near the lowest readings on record. The university's director of surveys, Joanne Hsu, said the decline was broad-based across political affiliation this month, with the sharpest drops among Democrats and Republicans alike (independents were little changed) — a sign this isn't a partisan-perception effect, but a genuine, broad reaction to gasoline prices and trade tensions. Perhaps most relevant for the Fed's next moves: year-ahead inflation expectations jumped a full 0.6 points to 4.6% in a single month, and five-year expectations — the number the Fed watches most closely for signs inflation psychology is becoming unanchored — also ticked up after holding steady for three straight months. Separately, the Conference Board's Leading Economic Index (a broader index combining 10 indicators) dipped 0.1% in August, also weighed down by weaker consumer expectations and declining building permits.

The Fed actually hiked, not just talked about it. On September 16, the FOMC raised its benchmark rate by a quarter point to 3.75%-4.00% — the first increase since July 2023 — unanimously, 12-0. The Fed's post-meeting statement said inflation "remains elevated" and cited persistent price pressure from the Iran war/energy shock and other factors tracked throughout this dashboard. The forecast, not just the decision: alongside the rate decision, the Fed released its quarterly Summary of Economic Projections — the "dot plot," where each official marks where they expect rates to be by year-end. This is the closest thing to an official Fed forecast that exists. Of 18 participants (Warsh does not submit a dot), 16 expect at least one more hike in 2026, and 4 of those expect two more; only 2 expect the Fed to hold here.
