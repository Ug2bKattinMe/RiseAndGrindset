A note on data reliability

Two things worth knowing before trusting any single month's number too much. First, the 2025 government shutdown caused BLS to skip publishing October and November 2025 CPI data entirely — those gaps distort some of the year-over-year comparisons still working through the system in 2026. Second, a federal watchdog report found that while BLS "took steps to fix data release failures," it concluded more safeguards are still needed — a reminder that even official releases carry some operational risk right now. None of this means the numbers below are wrong, just that they deserve the usual caution around revisions (CPI data can be revised for up to 5 years; jobs data gets revised monthly, sometimes by over 100,000 as happened with May/June 2026).

This is the measure that's easy to miss but directly affects money in people's pockets. The Chained CPI (C-CPI-U) — not the headline CPI everyone talks about — is what the IRS uses to set tax bracket thresholds and standard deductions each year, and a closely related index sets the annual Social Security Cost-of-Living Adjustment (COLA). It's designed to run slightly below headline CPI because it accounts for consumers substituting cheaper goods when prices rise (e.g., chicken instead of beef). At 3.3%, it's tracking close to headline CPI right now, which points toward a larger-than-average COLA bump for 2027 if the pattern holds through the fall reference period the Social Security Administration uses.
What this means for youIf you or someone you support relies on Social Security, this number (not headline CPI) is what determines next year's benefit increase. At 3.3%, expect a larger-than-typical COLA bump announced this October.

Update, Sept 18-19 — a second chokepoint is now compromised. Houthi forces (aligned with Iran) captured the Yemeni port city of Mokha this week, which overlooks the Bab el-Mandeb Strait — a separate waterway from Hormuz that Saudi Arabia relies on for oil exports via the Red Sea. Houthi fighters resumed targeting Saudi tankers there in solidarity with Iran's Hormuz blockade, and Saudi export traffic through the strait has fallen to under a third of this year's average. This had been a relatively contained, one-waterway crisis until this week; it no longer is. Separately, Iran claimed a new strike on an oil tanker attempting to transit Hormuz overnight (Sept 18), and reporting indicates the U.S. has asked South Korea to deploy troops near the Strait, drawing protests in Seoul — a sign the international military footprint here keeps expanding. President Trump was quoted saying the next move could be to "annihilate" the Iranian regime, a sharp escalation in rhetoric from his earlier, more measured comments about not expecting further escalation.
Update, Sept 7 (most recent confirmed data): Brent crude climbed to around $97/barrel, up 9% over five days and 19% over the month, as strikes continued to intensify. Shipping-tracking firm Kpler reported Strait traffic fell to an average of just 10 commodity ships per day over the prior 10 days — a sharp drop from the wartime-record 17 million barrels that moved through on September 1 under U.S. protection, illustrating how quickly conditions in the Strait can swing. U.S. forces reportedly hit three Iranian oil tankers over the weekend; Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed strikes on three tankers and three U.S.-linked vessels elsewhere. This is the most recent confirmed oil-market data as of this update — the situation remains active and unresolved heading into the Fed's rate decision and beyond (see Federal Reserve section).
Update, Sept 3-5: the conflict escalated further this week. Iran reportedly fired missiles at Kuwait, and Brent crude rose above $96/barrel — up more than 7% for the week — as the U.S. and Iran traded strikes for the first time since July. Energy Secretary Chris Wright said a wartime-record 17 million barrels of oil transited the Strait on September 1 under U.S. military protection, well above the roughly 5-8 million barrels/day this dashboard has cited through the summer, though still below the ~20 million barrels/day that moved through before the war. President Trump said Wednesday he doesn't expect the current fighting to escalate further, but that assessment should be read as one data point, not a resolution — this dashboard has now corrected its own "ceasefire" framing once already (see below).
Update, Sept 1: the escalation that began Aug 31 has continued to intensify. President Trump reportedly vowed to "hit them very hard" after Iran struck two U.S. bases in Jordan, and threats have now extended to Kharg Island — the terminal handling roughly 90% of Iran's oil exports (about 7 million barrels/day of capacity). Separately, refinery strikes inside Russia have tightened global refining capacity on top of the Hormuz disruption, and reporting indicates the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve has been drawn down close to minimum operational levels amid increased crude exports. A supertanker fire from a mine strike in the southern Strait, reported yesterday, has not been independently confirmed. This is now a broader supply-side story than the Strait alone — worth watching whether Kharg Island threats materialize, since that would be a significant step beyond the shipping-lane disruption this dashboard has tracked so far.
Update, August 31: fighting resumed today after several weeks of relative calm. U.S. forces struck Iranian rocket launchers near the Strait that were reportedly preparing to deploy mines, and Iran claimed retaliatory attacks on U.S. bases in Jordan; Iran also reported a supertanker struck two mines on Hormuz's southern route, though that's unconfirmed independently. Oil jumped in response (see Energy section above). This ends a period where oil had actually been trending down through most of August as diplomacy seemed possible — a reminder that this situation can reverse in a single day, in either direction.
Correction from an earlier version of this dashboard: a prior update described a "ceasefire" that reopened the Strait of Hormuz in late June. That was inaccurate. The Strait has instead been in a state of on-and-off crisis since Iranian forces effectively closed it on March 4, 2026 — insurance for ships transiting the Strait is unavailable or prohibitively expensive, and traffic remains severely disrupted, though analysts estimate 6-8 million barrels/day (down from a pre-war 20-24 million) still move through via other Gulf producers. Economists at CEPR estimate the disruption alone is adding roughly 0.6 percentage points to 2026 headline CPI and 0.2 points to core CPI — a meaningful, quantifiable share of the inflation tracked throughout this dashboard. This is a live, unresolved situation, not a resolved one, and energy figures above should be read with that in mind.

Wildfires don't move the national CPI the way oil shocks do, but they're a real, growing, and localized cost pressure — economists are calling the phenomenon "climateflation." The January 2025 Palisades and Eaton fires destroyed over 16,000 structures and produced roughly $40 billion in insured losses; nearly 400,000 California home insurance policies have been canceled since 2021, and FAIR Plan (the state's insurer of last resort) enrollment is up 43% in 15 months. Rebuilding costs are compounding the problem: construction material and skilled-labor shortages pushed residential construction wages up more than 9% in 2025, far outpacing overall inflation. The 2026 fire season is already tracking worse than most recent years, with Florida and Georgia in extreme drought and Colorado closing its worst snowpack year on record — meaning this cost pressure is not behind us. It shows up in this dashboard mainly through shelter and insurance costs in fire-prone regions, not the national CPI headline.

This is one of the steepest SNAP enrollment drops in about three decades — comparable to the 1996 welfare reform cuts. The cause is the "One Big Beautiful Bill" (H.R. 1), signed July 2025, which expanded work requirements and tightened eligibility, including for some immigrants with lawful status. The decline accelerated sharply after the law took effect: only about 744,000 people lost benefits in the first six months, versus millions in the months since. It's landed in every state except Alaska, with drops of 10%+ in 23 states.
The cause is genuinely disputed. USDA Secretary Brooke Rollins has attributed much of the decline to a strong economy and reduced fraud. Food-security researchers, and the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities (a left-leaning think tank that produces the most detailed state-level tracking), attribute it primarily to the program becoming harder to access — more paperwork, stricter work-requirement verification. This dashboard can't adjudicate that dispute, but the enrollment numbers themselves are drawn from USDA administrative data and aren't contested.
Why it belongs here: SNAP covers roughly 1 in 8 Americans, and its recipients are disproportionately exposed to the food-at-home inflation tracked in the Groceries section above. Losing benefits while grocery CPI is still running at 3.0% year-over-year compounds financial pressure for millions of households in a way the topline inflation numbers don't capture.

The two-month calm broke, and it's the same story as everywhere else on this dashboard right now: energy. PPI jumped 0.4% in August, pushing the annual rate back up to 5.4% from July's 4.7%. The Fed actually hiked, not just talked about it. On September 16, the FOMC raised its benchmark rate by a quarter point to 3.75%-4.00% — the first increase since July 2023 — unanimously, 12-0. The Fed's post-meeting statement said inflation "remains elevated" and cited persistent price pressure from the Iran war/energy shock and other factors tracked throughout this dashboard. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said plainly the Fed "cannot single-handedly stop price shocks on items like oil" but has a role in preventing that from spreading into broader inflation.
The forecast, not just the decision: alongside the rate decision, the Fed released its quarterly Summary of Economic Projections — the "dot plot," where each official marks where they expect rates to be by year-end. This is the closest thing to an official Fed forecast that exists. Of 18 participants (Warsh does not submit a dot), 16 expect at least one more hike in 2026, and 4 of those expect two more; only 2 expect the Fed to hold here. That points to a year-end target range of roughly 4.1%-4.4%, and futures markets are now pricing continued hikes extending into 2027. This is a genuinely different trajectory than this dashboard was tracking as recently as August, when the debate was still "cut or hold."
Context for how we got here: three regional bank presidents (Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari, Dallas's Lorie Logan) dissented in favor of a hike back in July. By September, the full committee agreed with them — driven by stalled core PCE (see PCE section above) and the hot August CPI report, both pointing the same direction.
